A cell starts on ? — nobody has confirmed that student yet, and until somebody does they count as absent. One tap says “I see you, you’re here”. After that the cell cycles present → absent → event → tardy → dismissed → back to present. Tapping one student changes nobody else; “Everyone’s here” is the one control that does, and that is what it says. Present is never stored — an empty cell on a day you have taken is present, and it is drawn as a P so it can’t be confused with a day nobody took. A whole column of amber question marks is a day you haven’t taken yet; a few of them inside a taken column are the students you haven’t reached, and the header counts them. A tardy or a dismissal keeps the time it was marked, under the letter. Tap the ⋯ by a name for that time, a note, or to put one student back to ?. Past days need their ✏ before they accept a tap; today’s column is always live, and its 🚫 says the class didn’t meet. Every tap saves as you make it; there is nothing to submit.

From the keyboard. On a laptop you never have to touch the mouse: press ↓ to land on the first name, then one letter per student — P present, T tardy, A absent, E event, D dismissed — and the selection moves down the list on its own, so a class of thirty is thirty keystrokes. Esc stops it. The whole list is on the ⌨ Keys button above, or press ?.

Passes. 🚽 bathroom, 🏥 nurse, ⚡ quick — one tap sends a student out and records the time they left; one tap on Return brings them back and writes the minutes down. Three students at a time, and the buttons grey out with the reason above the grid once you are at that limit. An open pass is stored, not remembered: close the app, drop the iPad, come back after lunch, and whoever is out is still out, with the time they left beside their name. A pass changes nobody’s attendance — a student at the bathroom was present — with one exception: marking someone D closes their pass, and taking the D back opens it again.

An assignment belongs to one class and one term, so this list is what is graded on and nothing else. Points, the category it counts in, and both dates are all editable afterwards — nothing here is settled by writing it down. A new assignment starts with both dates on today, because that is the day you are almost always writing it down on. Type over either one, or clear it and leave it blank — a date is never required, and clearing one leaves it empty. Nothing else is ever filled in for you. Planbook has no timetable and is not getting one, so there is no “next meeting” to guess at; a due date is a plain date you type, and it changes no grade and marks nobody. Duplicate copies an assignment into this class or into another one you teach — the dates come across as they are, and the copy arrives with no scores on it, which is the point of copying it rather than the cost.

Worth 0 points is how you give extra credit. A 0-point assignment scored 5 adds 5 to what a student has earned in its category and nothing to what was possible, so it lifts the grade instead of diluting it. There is no separate extra-credit switch and there is not going to be one.

Inside a number they still move the caret, so a score can be corrected without the mouse. Everything on this grid is , and nothing else. Every keystroke saves as you make it; there is nothing to submit, and Escape does nothing here because there is no dialog to close.

A blank cell is ungraded and changes no grade at all — it is not a zero. Clearing a score back to blank takes it out of the book entirely. Missing is the one that counts as zero, and it is only ever marked by you: nothing in Planbook decides it from a due date. Excused drops the work out of the grade completely, and late changes nothing about the arithmetic — it is a record you can see, not a penalty. Each of the three shows in the cell, in the fill and in a corner letter, because a score that silently isn't what you typed is the worst thing a gradebook can do.

The grade beside each name is live, and it is the weighted average of this class's categories with the empty ones' weight spread across the ones that have work. It can go over 100%: an assignment worth 0 points is how extra credit works here, and nothing caps what a student earned. Until the weights total 100% there is no grade at all — not a provisional one — and the banner above says what they come to. Nothing is blocked while they are wrong: keep entering scores, and the grades appear the moment the weights do.

About Planbook

Planbook is a gradebook and communication assistant. It tracks grades and attendance, surfaces the students who need attention in both directions — concern and praise — and drafts the outreach that follows.

Everything stays on this device. There is no account and no server: your data lives in this browser's own storage, and Google Drive sync is an opt-in extra you can ignore entirely.

This build

Attendance, your roster, and how each class is graded are what you can enter so far. The frame and the storage behind it are real — one document per school year, kept in this browser — and so are the class bar at the top and the students behind it. Marking a class is finished and in daily use, hall passes and the days a class didn't meet included.

The gradebook itself is half here. You can set up what a class is graded on, what each part is worth, and the assignments themselves — a class now has an Assignments screen beside its attendance, with points, categories and dates on it. What is still missing is the half that carries numbers: there are no scores yet and no grades, so nothing in Planbook works out a percentage for anybody. That is what comes next. Signals, outreach and Drive sync come after it.

The backup file that gets everything back out of the browser is already here, under the ⤓ button in the header, because nothing that stores a student's name ships before the way to get it back.

Call it whatever you call it out loud — “Period 3 — Biology”, “AP Bio”, “3rd hour”. Categories & weights

What is graded on, and how much each part counts. Every class has its own set, so one class weighted 40/35/25 and another weighted evenly is an ordinary thing here — Planbook never makes them match.

Categories in this class

Weights are percentages of the final grade and want to add up to 100. They do not have to right now — nothing is blocked while they are wrong, and Planbook will say what they come to until they add up. A category with no work in it yet doesn’t drag the grade down: its weight spreads across the categories that do have work, so an empty one costs nothing.

To stop a category counting without losing anything, set its weight to 0 — the assignments stay and the grade stops using them. Removing is the one that takes the work with it, which is why it counts what goes first.

Letter grades

A percentage takes the first band it reaches, reading down this list. So the boundary you type is the whole of the rule: if 89.5 should be an A, type 89.5 — nothing here rounds a percentage before it looks for a letter, and there is no second rule to disagree with your school’s system about.

These bands are for
Bands, highest first

Each band runs from its own boundary up to the next one above it, which is what the range beside it says — 93 belongs to the band that starts at 93, not to the one below. That is why there is only one number to type per band, and why two bands can never leave a hole between them. Nothing is blocked while a scale is wrong: keep going, and the line above says what it makes of the bands as they are.

A class only has its own bands if you give it them; otherwise it uses the ones above, and changing those changes every class at once. Putting a class back on the year’s bands discards the ones you typed for it — nothing else in that class is touched, because a letter scale holds no grades.

Signal thresholds

When Planbook should tell you about a student, in both directions. Concern is half of it: the other half is the student who has turned something around, and a student can be on both lists at once — a grade climbing while attendance slips is exactly who you want to see twice.

Windows count recorded meetings, not days. A class can go a week without meeting, so “the last 20 meetings” is a real stretch of this class’s history and “the last 20 days” would not be. The three settings measured in days say so on their own rows: each of those is time since something was written down — an email, a behavior note — rather than a run of lessons.

These numbers are stored with this school year, not on this device, so they follow your gradebook to your other devices and into every backup. Nothing is blocked while you tune them, and no student is flagged by a number you cannot see on this screen.

The concern and praise lists themselves are not built yet — these are the numbers they will read. Setting them now costs nothing and changes nothing on any screen.

Outreach

Backup & restore

Planbook keeps each school year in this browser's own storage. That storage can be erased — by iPadOS after a week away, by a browser reset, by a laptop that stops starting — so a file you download and keep somewhere safe is the real safety net. Download one at the end of every week.

Download a backup

What is in this file. Everything Planbook holds for that school year: your classes, your roster, guardian and counselor contacts, attendance, grades, and your record of who you have contacted. It also contains the support details you keep on students — accommodations, IEP and 504 plans, case managers and review dates, medical needs, and behavior plans. That is on purpose: a backup that left them out would not bring your gradebook back.

It is plain text, so anyone who opens the file can read all of it. Keep it the way you keep a paper folder of the same information — somewhere only you can reach, and not in an email.

Restore from a backup

Restoring replaces one whole school year with the copy in the file — the year named inside the file, not whichever year you happen to have open. Planbook shows you what is being replaced and asks before it changes anything.
